For universities, colleges and academic institutions

The next academic advantage

Courses teach what is known. The future will reward students who can see what is not.

Degrees, exams, papers and patents still matter. But the next differentiator is deeper: can a student notice an important problem, frame it sharply, think beyond the obvious, test an idea rigorously and create something worth defending?

That ability is often called “innovation”. It can be taught as a disciplined capability that helps institutions develop stronger problem-finders, researchers and inventors.

The real gap

Innovation is not “think of a creative idea”.

That simplification is exactly why many innovation sessions create energy for a day and very little capability afterwards.

WHAT STUDENTS OFTEN HEAR

“Think outside the box.”

But nobody explains how to find the box, which constraint matters, why the problem is worth solving, or how to test whether an idea is actually novel.

WHAT THEY NEED TO PRACTISE

Observe → Frame → Diverge → Challenge → Validate → Build → Defend

A repeatable thinking discipline that works across engineering, AI, healthcare, business, design and research.

The Problem-to-Proof Method

Move students from “give me a problem statement” to “I found a problem worth solving.”

The program uses real situations, contradictions, stakeholder tensions and emerging technology to make innovative thinking practical rather than motivational.

01NOTICE

See unresolved tensions

Observe users, systems, workflows and societal friction. Learn to notice what others normalise.

02FRAME

Ask a sharper question

Turn a broad theme into a precise problem with beneficiaries, constraints and measurable value.

03BREAK PATTERNS

Generate non-obvious directions

Use contradiction thinking, reverse assumptions, analogies and structured creativity instead of random brainstorming.

04TEST

Challenge the idea

Check novelty, feasibility, evidence, ethics, adoption and what would make the idea fail.

05BUILD

Create evidence

Define a small experiment, prototype, study or research plan that can make the idea stronger or kill it early.

06DEFEND

Explain why it matters

Communicate the problem, insight, evidence and contribution to reviewers, employers, juries and decision-makers.

The outcome:students who can enter interviews, hackathons, research labs and project reviews with better questions, stronger reasoning and evidence-backed ideas.Explore the program →
Research excellence track

Do not begin with “How do I publish this?” Begin with “Why would a selective journal or conference care?”

A literature gap alone is not automatically an important research problem. Strong papers usually begin much earlier, with a consequential question, a defensible contribution and evidence designed to survive expert scrutiny.

I guide students and research cohorts through the part that is often skipped: choosing the problem, mapping the state of the art, understanding venue expectations, testing novelty, designing evidence and shaping a clear contribution.

01Problem before paper

Find a burning, consequential question instead of stretching a convenient dataset into a paper.

02Venue-aware reading

Study what leading journals and conferences actually reward before deciding the contribution.

03Novelty under pressure

Ask what is genuinely new, non-obvious and useful, and what a skeptical reviewer could reject.

04Evidence that carries the claim

Align experiments, baselines, ablations, validation and limitations with the strength of the proposed contribution.

05Selective-venue ambition

Aim beyond “publish somewhere”. Build work that is better positioned for strong, selective journals and conferences.

Quality over publication count.

Publication pressure can encourage incremental work, poor venue fit or weak research practices. Retractions are a global research-integrity problem, not an Indian identity. My goal is to help students move away from “complete a paper” thinking and toward work that can withstand stronger methodological and reviewer scrutiny.

From insight to IP

Some ideas should become papers. Some should become patents. Students should learn to tell the difference.

Patent thinking trains a different intellectual muscle: novelty, non-obviousness, claims, prior art and the ability to describe an invention as a system rather than only an implementation.

Prior-art thinking

Search beyond keywords and understand what has already been claimed.

Novelty vs non-obviousness

Separate “different” from “inventive enough to matter”.

Paper or patent?
