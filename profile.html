About Dr Amar Banerjee

I work where difficult problems, research and invention meet.

Across more than twelve years, my work has moved through control engineering, knowledge systems, digital twins, industrial AI, healthcare AI, enterprise data science and academic innovation. The common thread is simple: understand the problem deeply, make the reasoning explicit, and turn the insight into something that can be tested, taught, published, patented or used.

That is also how I work with students. I do not separate innovative thinking, research quality and patent thinking into three disconnected activities. They are different outcomes of the same intellectual habit: finding a consequential problem and developing a defensible contribution.

What connects the work

A career built around making complex engineering knowledge usable.

My early research focused on control systems and the question of whether engineering knowledge could be represented explicitly enough to improve reuse, modelling and software generation. That led naturally into domain-specific languages, knowledge-centric engineering environments, simulation, robotics and control synthesis.

The same idea later expanded into digital twins and cyber-physical systems: if a system can describe its resources, capabilities and behaviour clearly, software and control solutions can become easier to generate, adapt and explain.

More recently, the work has included applied AI, responsible AI, healthcare and scientific computing, LLM systems, enterprise data science and Green AI. Across these areas, I remain interested in the same deeper question: how do we make intelligent systems useful without losing the reasoning, evidence and engineering discipline behind them?

For academic institutions, this experience becomes a teaching proposition. Students should learn not only how to use tools, but how to identify important problems, test novelty, design evidence, understand publication venues and recognise when an idea may have patent potential.

Why invention matters in the academic programs

Patent thinking is not about turning every student project into an application. It teaches students to search prior art, distinguish novelty from non-obviousness, recognise the essential inventive concept and make a deliberate paper-vs-patent decision before public disclosure.
